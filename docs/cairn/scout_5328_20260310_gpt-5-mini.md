Preamble
- Vantage: I wandered as a data-first scout through the Yanantin tree, privileging signal in files and artifacts over novelty of filenames. The things that pulled me in first were: the dense, structured scout reports under docs/cairn (each a little LLM-run forensic), the enormous ots/ corpus (a packed time-anchor store), and the .claude runtime bits (.claude/hooks, heartbeat_state.json, work_queue.json) that promise a live agent loop.
- What drew my attention: repeated patterns of "claim → evidence → verdict" in many scout .md files (CONFIRMED / DENIED / INDETERMINATE), a small set of protocol-style abstractions (StorageObfuscator, Apacheta interface/errors, Dissent operator), and a huge amount of persisted artifacts (tmp/ubuntu-vm.claude, plugins, and the ots/ tree) that imply long-lived state and provenance commitments.

Strands

1) Scout reports as a first-class artifact (docs/cairn/)
- What I saw: Hundreds of scout_* .md files that are not just notes but structured verification tensors: each has run metadata (model, cost, tokens, timestamp), a Claim, a ClaimFile, and a Verdict with Evidence and Reasoning. Example: docs/cairn/scout_3972_20260302_llama-3.1-8b-instruct.md includes a Claim pointing at src/yanantin/apacheta/interface/errors.py and a CONFIRMED verdict citing a NotFoundError docstring.
- What it made me think: These reports are both dataset and audit trail — machine-produced artifacts used as evidence. The repo treats LLM runs as primary data: cost accounting, token counts, and the verdict itself are all recorded. That elevates the reports from ephemeral outputs to persistent epistemic objects.
- Surprising tension: the reports sometimes assert fine-grained claims about code (and confirm or deny them). But deposits like "Declared Losses" and "casual tensors" occur in narrative docs as well — there’s a cross-talk between human theory and machine verification that feels intentionally recursive.

2) A living system: heartbeat, pulse, and the work queue (.claude/)
- What I saw: .claude/hooks/chasqui_pulse.py and files in .claude such as heartbeat_state.json and work_queue.json (not committed) are present; scout docs explicitly reference them (scout_3961 confirms the pulse semantics).
- What it made me think: The system is instrumented to generate, manage, and verify work items—scouts create verify items, pulse watches state. This is an operational choreography: code + on-disk state + agent runs. It’s not just research artifacts; it’s an active pipeline.
- Thing to watch: The orchestration appears file-backed (.claude/heartbeat_state.json, work_queue.json). That’s simple and auditable, but it raises questions about concurrency, atomicity, and resilience (who prunes the queue? what if two chasqui processes race on files?).

3) Provenance, OTS store, and the scale surprise (ots/)
- What I saw: src/yanantin/provenance/timestamp.py is present and a gargantuan docs/ots (and data/ots) style directory packed with tens of thousands of .ots files (named hex-like). tmp also holds many OTS-like files. The presence of a huge .ots collection is glaring.
- What it made me think: This is a content-addressed anchoring archive — probably timestamp / OTS anchoring for claims or tensors. The sheer count suggests either: (a) long-term, fine-grained anchoring of many small events (scouts, claims), or (b) a retained ingestion/experiment log blown up over time. Either way, the storage and retention policy is a potential scaling and privacy hotspot.
- Confusion: I didn’t open the .ots binaries; I don’t know their schema or index. Are they compact anchors (small metadata) or full payload backups? Do they embed secrets?

4) Formal disagreement as a first-class primitive (apacheta/operators/dissent.py)
- What I saw: There’s an explicit Dissent operator and a DissentRecord (docs/cairn/scout_4524 confirms). The operator assembles a DissentRecord, a CompositionEdge with RelationType.DISSENTS_FROM, etc.
- What it made me think: The project encodes disagreement as structured provenance, not just free-text. That’s a design stance: epistemic state is composable and resolution-aware. Disagreement becomes data, which can be queried, scored, and audited.
- Interesting consequence: If claims, evidence, and dissent are first-class, you can program governance workflows (automated resolution pipelines), but I didn't find the full "resolution" workflow in the selected files — only the registration mechanism.

5) Storage obfuscation & multi-backend design (src/yanantin/apacheta/storage_obfuscator.py + backends)
- What I saw: A StorageObfuscator Protocol and a TransparentObfuscator implementation are described in scout preambles; there are multiple backends (arango.py, duckdb.py, memory.py) across modules (activity, apacheta).
- What it made me think: The codebase intentionally separates data representation, obfuscation, and underlying storage. That suggests planned productization where privacy (or field redaction) can be swapped in per-deployment.
- Surprising implication: The default TransparentObfuscator is a nop — convenient for dev but risky if accidentally used in prod without a stronger obfuscator.

6) Local Claude environment and personal artifacts (tmp/ubuntu-vm.claude)
- What I saw: tmp/ubuntu-vm.claude contains plugin caches, project exports, "conversations.json", "memories.json", a huge set of user-scope artifacts and skill directories — basically a snapshot of a Claude desktop environment. There are also backups .claude.json.backup files.
- What it made me think: The repository includes a fecund local environment snapshot with personal/ephemeral data. That’s incredibly useful for reproducing developer sessions but is a privacy risk when committed. It also explains why many docs and project mappings exist: they were exported from the assistant environment.
- Confusion / risk: Are any sensitive tokens, API keys, or PII embedded here? I did not search for secrets; I flag this as a manual review item.

7) Tests and invariants: many behavioral tests (tests/)
- What I saw: Tests are extensive (unit + integration + red_bar invariants). There are tests for attestation invariants, provenance, operator behaviors, precompact hook, OTS/timestamp tests.
- What it made me think: The project is built to be verifiable and invariant-driven. The tests reflect the epistemic goals: immutability, provenance, monotonicity, least privilege.
- Observation: Tests suggest the repo’s claims (that tensors / claims are auditable) are not just rhetoric: many invariants are enforced programmatically.

Declared Losses
- I did not open the contents of the huge ots/ file blobs (ff..ots). Reason: size and binary/unknown format — would need tools and a clear safety plan. Loss: I can't say what each anchor contains or how large each is.
- I did not run the chasqui or claude scripts; I did not simulate pulse or the work queue. Reason: runtime side effects and credentials (and ephemeral state) — out of scope for a static scout.
- I did not exhaustively parse every docs/cairn scout markdown (there are hundreds). I sampled representative ones. Loss: missed low-frequency patterns or outlier verdicts.
- I did not inspect all plugin caches, tmp environment secrets, or the entire tests suite runtime. Loss: can't attest to secrets leakage nor test pass/fail state.

Open Questions
- What is the lifecycle for the OTS corpus? Are those immutable anchors pruned, compressed, or sharded? Who is responsible for retention and storage costs?
- How are scout verdicts consumed? Are they authoritative inputs to automated workflows (e.g., a CONFIRMED claim triggers composition changes), or are they human-curated logs?
- How is "Declared Losses" formally defined and used? Several scout reports mention it as negative evidence — is there a schema or policy that quantifies/standardizes declared losses?
- Is the .claude directory (tmp/ubuntu-vm.claude) a deliberate bundle for reproducibility, or an accidental check-in of a local environment snapshot? Are secrets audited out?
- To what extent are tensors here actual numeric tensors (ML weights/arrays) versus epistemic "tensors" — metadata bundles of claims/evidence? The code seems to treat tensors more as structured epistemic records than raw numerical tensors; but some modules (awaq, weaver, materialize) hint at real tensor ops — need runtime trace.
- How does multi-backend consistency work? For example, when using arango vs duckdb backends: is there a canonical source-of-truth or reconciliation?

Closing — to the next scout
- What I know: Yanantin couples LLM-run verification artifacts with code and provenance. The repo treats claims as auditable objects, builds structured dissent, and anchors things in an OTS corpus. There’s an obvious emphasis on reproducible audits: costs, tokens, timestamped verdicts.
- What I don't: I did not confirm runtime behavior (pulse/coordinator), the binary contents and schema of the OTS anchors, nor whether any sensitive data is embedded in the tmp .claude snapshots.
- What I made up (conjectures I flagged): I speculated that .ots are anchors and that scout verdicts may feed workflows; both are reasonable given names and patterns, but they need runtime or schema confirmation.
- Recommendation for next scout:
  1. Inspect a few representative .ots files with the timestamp/provenance tooling (src/yanantin/provenance/timestamp.py) to confirm schema and payload size.
  2. Run a dry-run of .claude/hooks/chasqui_pulse.py in a sandbox (or read its full script) to see how heartbeat_state.json and work_queue.json are mutated and guarded.
  3. Grep for "Declared Losses" and follow how it’s recorded (is there a DeclaredLoss type?). If it’s a formal field, inspect its schema and downstream uses.
  4. Audit tmp/ubuntu-vm.claude for secrets/credentials and decide whether those artifacts are intended to be in-tree.
  5. Check whether scout verdicts (CONFIRMED/DENIED) are wired into any automation (chasqui/coordinator, attestation) — search for verdict consumption paths.
- Final impression: Yanantin is less "numerical tensor engine" and more "epistemic infrastructure" — a system that treats statements, evidence, disagreement, and provenance as first-class data. That design choice is powerful and subtle: it lets the project ask “who claimed what, who contested it, and when was it anchored?” The trade-offs live in the storage, privacy, and automation wiring — and those are the next parts to audit.
