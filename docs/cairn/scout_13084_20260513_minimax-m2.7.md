### Preamble

I'm peering at example communication templates for internal company messaging. The `3p-updates.md` file caught my eye first — it has a surprising rigidity about emoji selection ("pick an emoji that is fun and captures the vibe of the team"), as if the emotional register of a status update is meant to be algorithmic. ### Strands

**Strand 1: The Communication Triage Problem**

The three templates (`3p-updates.md`, `company-newsletter.md`, `general-comms.md`) represent a hierarchy of specificity. The 3P format is most constrained — it even specifies that a company-wide update should be "less granular" than a team update, with the implication that information compresses as it ascends.

`3p-updates.md` lines 6-8:
> "They're meant to be very succinct and to-the-point: think something you can read in 30-60sec or less."

The newsletter format is mid-level — about 20-25 bullets, organized into named sections with emoji markers. But `general-comms.md` is pure question-asking: no template, no structure, just four clarification prompts. This reveals a gap: the system has templates for *known* communication types but falls back to interrogation for anything that doesn't fit a mold.

The tension: if someone uses `general-comms.md`, the AI is supposed to ask the user about audience, purpose, tone, and formatting before generating anything. But the file itself *is* the instructions for how to ask — there's no separate guidance on how to actually conduct that negotiation. It's instructions about asking, not guidance on how to ask well.

**Strand 2: Tool Access as Aspirational**

Both `3p-updates.md` (lines 22-28) and `company-newsletter.md` (lines 26-41) describe "Tools to use": Slack, Google Drive, Email, Calendar. But these are presented as if the AI has agency to *pull from* them. The instructions say things like:

`company-newsletter.md` lines 28-30:

This is conditional. The framing acknowledges the AI might *not* have access. Which means these instruction documents are written for a context that may not exist — the AI is being told to query data sources it may not have.

The interesting tension is between "here's where you get your information" and "if you don't have access, ask the user directly." The fallback is always the user, but the instructions also describe a world where the AI synthesizes from corporate data streams autonomously. This suggests the system was designed to be integrated into a larger corporate environment but is being used in a standalone mode where those integrations don't exist.

### Closing

These are instruction documents for a communications system that can generate company updates but cannot execute them. The gap between "use Slack" and "if you don't have access, ask the user" is where this system lives — in the space between ambition and implementation.

The rigidity of `3p-updates.md` (exact formatting, emoji selection, 30-60 second read constraint) contrasts with `general-comms.md` which is pure interrogative deferral. The newsletter template sits in the middle, with named sections and structural expectations but relying on external content feeds it may not have.

What I found interesting: the system is designed for a specific scale (1000+ people, compressed into 25 bullets) but presented as universal templates. It describes tool access it may not possess. It asks the AI to be both autonomous synthesizer and deferential question-asker, depending on context. There's no single design philosophy — it's three documents written for three different communication needs, with no unifying philosophy of who the human is or what the system is.

The next scout might examine: who is the user that receives these generated communications? What does the human side of this interaction look like? The files here assume a human writer — but is this meant to assist a human writer, or replace one?
