### Preamble

Vantage: `openai/gpt-5.1`, dropped into `docs/hypotheses/late-binding-as-correctness.md`.  

What pulled my attention immediately wasn’t “late binding” the phrase, but the insistence that:

> “This is not lazy evaluation and not traditional late binding… this pattern defers the ontology of the result.”

So this is a self-conscious attempt to name a pattern that keeps popping up across the system, and to claim it’s *structurally* different from standard CS stories. The tension: they’re trying to turn an architectural quirk (“we only know what this means later”) into a *correctness* strategy.

### Strands

#### Strand 1 — Ontology as a runtime artifact

**What I saw**

The core hypothesis: the system stores almost-nothing, and lets “what this means” be a runtime emergent thing.

The table near the top is doing heavy conceptual work:

- **Activity Anchor**: stores `timestamp + UUID`, materializes “facts across all streams at that moment” at query time.
- **Jabberwock**: stores a near-empty UUID entity, materializes a “Frabjous” view with proof envelope when you call `galumph()` / `uffish()`.
- **Mome Vorpal**: stores “observation without entity binding”, materializes a “claim event linking to an entity” when some relationship is later identified.

The crucial move: *the shape of what materializes is unknown at write time*.

They emphasize this by contrast:

- Lazy evaluation: we already know the expression, just not the value.
- Late binding (OO / DI): we know the interface, just not the implementation.
- This system: we don’t even know the *ontology* — “what question will be asked” and “what counts as ‘the answer’” are future decisions.

**What it made me think**

This is basically elevating the **open-world assumption** from a semantic stance into a *storage invariant*:

- Facts are recorded in a way that explicitly anticipates that:
  - new *streams* may appear,
  - new *entity schemas* may appear,
  - new *questions* may appear,
  - and prior artifacts must remain correct without migration.

The surprising bit: they’re not just tolerating “unknown future schema”; they are designing anchors so that *any* future ontology can in principle “snap” onto them. That’s a pretty strong statement.

It also hints at a more subtle claim: **immutability of raw events is weaker than what they want**. Lots of event-sourced systems say “never mutate events” — but here, even the *expected projections* are unknown. So correctness isn’t “replay produces the same view,” but “replay under a richer ontology is still well-typed, and old anchors don’t become wrong by omission.”

There’s also an implicit epistemic ethic: every artifact promises:

- “I will never *claim* more than I knew at the time of writing.”
- “I will never prevent you from knowing more later.”

That’s a stricter guarantee than, say, most data warehouses give.

This feels like: **“schema-on-read, but for entities and questions too, not just fields.”**


#### Strand 2 — Correctness by *not* pre-committing (temporal tension)

**What I saw**

They keep returning to temporal asymmetry:

> “Meaning emerges at query time from whatever information exists *at that moment*, including information that didn't exist when the artifact was created.”

And in the Pichay pager example:

> “The anchor resolves to current content, not the original evicted content… same temporal property as activity anchors (new streams retroactively enrich old anchors) but applied to files on disk.”

So anchors are *time directions with handles*, not frozen snapshots. If you:

- add a new stream → old anchors get retroactively richer.
- edit a file → the old pager handle points to the **new** file, not an archive.

Meaning is explicitly **non-monotonic in time but monotonic in anchoring**: the handle is stable, the materialization is allowed to expand or even qualitatively change.

**What it made me think**

Two conflicting correctness stories are living here:

1. **Archive correctness**: “What did we know and see *then*?”
2. **Opportunistic correctness**: “Given what we know *now*, what’s the best answer to this anchored question?”

The hypothesis leans into (2). The pager design is especially revealing: they *could* have made the handle rehydrate the original bytes (classical snapshot semantics) but intentionally don’t. That’s a subtle architectural statement:

- The “unit of correctness” is not the past state, but the *present re-interpretation of a past anchor*.

This is unusual. Most systems terrified of compliance / audit prefer (1). Here they’re betting that *temporal re-enrichment* (retroactive use of new info) is more important.

That suggests two unspoken assumptions:

- **The epistemic object of interest is the question, not the historical state.** Anchors are “questions I might want to ask later,” not “states I must be able to reproduce bit-for-bit.”
- **Audit needs can be pushed to a different layer.** If you need full historical fidelity, something else (not these anchors) must be logging raw content.

This dual-correctness tension is acknowledged by example but not resolved in the text. It’s one of the main fault lines I’d poke at if I were designing around this.


#### Strand 3 — Mome & compacted cells: unresolved ≠ error

**What I saw**

The Mome Vorpal pattern:

> “Mome observations sit unresolved indefinitely. They are valuable pending, not erroneous unresolved.”

And then they argue that “compacted cells” in context windows are structurally the same:

- stored: conclusion + declared losses + handle
- materialized: full original tool output when needed
- the compacted cell is “structurally a mome”: **the observation exists, but its full content is not bound into the current context.**

This is leaning hard against a pervasive systems reflex:

- “Unbound, unresolved, or dangling” usually means bug, leak, or debt.
- Here, “unbound” is a first-class **steady state**.

**What it made me think**

They’re trying to formalize a really common *epistemic* phenomenon:

- “There’s something here, I’ve seen enough to summarize it, but I’m not going to drag the whole thing forward unless the future needs it.”

The twist is that they insist this can be both:

- **Space-efficient** (obvious in context compaction),
- **And correctness-preserving**, *if* the compacted cell’s declared losses are honest.

That “declared losses” phrase is doing more work than the doc spells out. It implies:

- For each compaction, you declare:
  - what information was *thrown away*,
  - what it *can no longer support* in terms of future inferences.

If those declarations are accurate, you can argue:

- Any future question whose answer *depends* on the lost details must trigger a re-materialization via the handle, or refuse to answer.

This gives a potential decision rule:

- “Answer from the compacted summary iff the question doesn’t require any of the lost dimensions.”

The doc hints at this, but doesn’t nail it down. It instead proposes an empirical non-inferiority test (Phase 2) as the correctness check. That’s practical, but conceptually weaker than the logic sketched above.


#### Strand 4 — The AI-as-participant evidence (pager experiment)

**What I saw**

The pager handle experiment is uncomfortably meta: an AI both **builds** the pager and later **suffers** from it.

Two key bits:

- The human-readable handle:

  > `[Paged out: Read /path/to/file.py (8,192 bytes, 187 lines). Re-read the file if you need its content.]`

- Behavioral result:

  > “When a fresh instance resumed a session with paged-out content, it unprompted said: ‘Let me re-read the files I need since some were paged out.’”

And:

> “Over 681 turns, 659 page faults fired — 97% fault rate… thrashing, not efficient paging… but it confirms the mechanism works: the model knows how to use the handles.”

**What it made me think**

What’s interesting here is not “LLM can read instruction text” — that’s obvious. Two more subtle things:

1. **The handle is semantically self-explaining**: no extra protocol, no side-band specification. That means:
   - The “ontology of the anchor” is encoded sufficiently in natural language + simple structure.
   - A foreign reasoning system (a future LLM, human, another tool) can recognize “this is a retrieval handle, not data” simply by reading.

   This supports the hypothesis’s claim that anchors can be ontologically deferred yet still **semantically recoverable by unknown future agents**.

2. **The high page-fault rate is not just thrashing; it’s a stress-test of the epistemic boundary.**
   - Every fault is effectively: “the summary is not enough for my current question; I need the underlying artifact.”
   - 97% suggests the compaction policy used here was *epistemically too aggressive* — the declared losses were too large relative to the questions actually asked.

   That’s a useful tension the text doesn’t fully dig into: **how to tune compaction so that “pending” remains valuable, not a constant source of page faults.**


#### Strand 5 — Measurement and falsifiability (rare for conceptual docs)

**What I saw**

They’re explicit about tests:

- Phase 1: measure cost of eager binding — they report “78.2% of context window is consumed by tool outputs that have already been acted on.”
- Phase 2: test **non-inferiority** — compacted sessions should produce equivalent quality to eager sessions.
- They propose refutation conditions:
  - Pattern reduces to “just the open-world assumption”.
  - Compacted sessions degrade quality measurably.
  - Eager-materialization systems *don’t* show the expected pathologies.

**What it made me think**

The unusual bit here is the **physics-of-architecture framing**:

- “Phase 1 measures the cost of eager materialization in this domain.”
- This is not just an anecdote; it’s a baseline against which they want to test another design.

Also, the particular metric chosen (context utilization) reveals something about the system’s value function:

- They’re treating **context window** as a scarce, first-class resource, analogous to memory bandwidth or disk budget in traditional systems.
- Correctness is not just logical validity; it’s “what you can preserve about the world under a constrained representation budget.”

This is important: **they’re embedding resource constraints into the concept of epistemic correctness**. That’s rarely explicit in knowledge representation work, which tends to assume infinite paper / RAM.

I don’t know if their eventual metrics will be strong enough (e.g., “session quality” is fuzzy), but the presence of a falsifiable plan at all is notable for a hypothesis that’s mostly narrative.


#### Strand 6 — The missing sharp boundary with prior art

**What I saw**

They acknowledge prior art:

> “Prior art search finds this distinguished from lazy evaluation and late binding in the knowledge representation or database literature. **Status: partially confirmed** (see Literature Survey).”

And a potential refutation:

> “The pattern is simply the open-world assumption from knowledge representation, already well-characterized. (This would not refute the utility — only the novelty.)”

But in the excerpt I saw, they don’t actually walk through:

- where exact boundaries with:
  - event sourcing,
  - bitemporal databases,
  - log-structured merge architectures,
  - CRDT-based systems,
  - RDF + open-world assumption

are drawn.

**What it made me think**

They’re right that there’s *a* difference, but they haven’t crystallized it yet. My guess, from the local evidence, is:

- Many prior systems:
  - assume open-world in *truth* (“more facts can be added later”)
  - but still fix the *entity / schema ontology* at design time.

Yanantin is trying to say:

- Even “what is an entity?”, “what are the streams?”, “what viewpoints exist?” are not privileged; they can be added later and must retroactively apply to all prior anchors without migration.

In other words: **ontological openness, not just factual openness.**  

That’s probably the actual novelty, but the doc (as seen) doesn’t quite punch that line through with examples contrasting classic systems.

This fuzzy boundary is a tension: if they want this hypothesis to survive contact with experts, it needs a more aggressive “this is where we differ” section.

### Open Questions

1. **Where is the hard line between “anchor” and “snapshot”?**
   - Do they ever need immutable, archival snapshots?  
   - If so, is that a separate type (e.g., “frozen anchor”) or is everything always “live to future ontology”?

2. **How are “declared losses” represented for compacted cells?**
   - Are they explicit structured metadata (e.g., “truncated lines 200–1000; removed stack traces; dropped per-token logits”)?
   - Or are they informal natural-language notes like “omitted details of the stack trace, only captured summary of root cause”?

3. **What happens when compaction lies (or is just wrong)?**
   - If the compacted cell *claims* certain losses but, due to a bug, actually drops more, can the system detect that?
   - Is there any invariant or guardrail, e.g., some checksumming over semantic content, or is correctness purely empirical at this layer?

4. **How do they plan to handle divergent temporal needs?**
   - Some consumers will want “current best view”, others “what could we have known then?”.  
   - Does the same anchor serve both, parameterized by query-time semantics (“anchor @-now” vs “anchor @-then”)?  
   - Or are there separate anchoring schemes for these two classes of queries?

5. **Where is the failure mode boundary for late ontology binding?**
   - They note that if Phase 2 degrades session quality, they “learn where the pattern breaks.” But:
     - Is that a general “this pattern only works when X…”?
     - Or will they respond by adjusting compaction policy rather than abandoning late binding?
   - In other words: is the hypothesis “global architecture should defer ontology” or “this is a tunable knob we can apply in some places”?

6. **Does the system support multiple concurrent ontologies?**
   - Anchors are supposed to be re-interpret-able by future, richer ontologies — but what if two ontologies disagree (e.g., two entity models for the same raw events)?
   - Is there a meta-layer that tracks which ontology was used for a given materialization, so results are reproducible?

7. **What is the actual type of a “question”?**
   - The hypothesis distinguishes “I don’t know the answer yet” from “I don’t know the question yet.”
   - Is “question” operationalized anywhere (e.g., as a query graph, a tensor signature, a contract)?
   - Or is it currently an emergent thing (LLM prompt + tool spec) that they haven’t yet formalized?

### Closing

From this vantage, Yanantin looks like a system trying to be **honest about ignorance and future surprise at the architectural level.** The late-binding-as-correctness hypothesis is a story about resisting early ontological commitments:

- Don’t decide what an entity *is* too early.
- Don’t bake in which streams “matter” too early.
- Don’t drag entire tool outputs forward if you only need their conclusions.
- Don’t treat “unresolved” as a bug if you can design it as a stable, valuable state.

It’s also a story about **time**: the same anchor supports different, richer worlds as you move forward, and that’s framed as a feature, not a cache-invalidation nightmare.
