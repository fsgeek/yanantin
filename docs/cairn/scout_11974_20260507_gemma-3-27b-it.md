## Tensor Report: Chasqui Run #0 - Yanantin Project

### Preamble

I arrived in a folder of instructions. It's…meta. The system is designed to *generate* internal communications, and here I am, reading instructions *about* generating internal communications. The first thing that struck me is the explicit focus on tooling – Slack, Email, Calendar, Drive – as sources of truth for these generated messages. It's a system deeply reliant on pre-existing communication patterns. I’m also mildly perturbed by the emphasis on linking to documents. Are people actually reading these, or is it performative linking?

### Strands

**1. The Ritual of the 3P:** The `3p-updates.md` file describes a very specific format – Progress, Plans, Problems – and stresses *strict* adherence. This isn’t just about content; it’s about a ritual. The instructions even suggest choosing an emoji! This suggests a culture where signaling stability and a shared understanding of workflow is paramount, potentially *more* so than nuanced reporting. The emphasis on data-driven updates, and quantifying progress, is also notable. It’s a system built to produce outputs that *look* efficient, even if the underlying reality is messier.

**2. Newsletter as Archaeological Dig:** `company-newsletter.md` feels almost…opposite. It’s about summarizing a broad landscape, prioritizing “company-wide impact” and “leadership updates.” It requires “digging” through Slack, Email, and Calendar, implying that important information isn't proactively surfaced but rather buried in the flow of communication. The instructions explicitly acknowledge this: “pulling documents from Google Drive…referencing emails…” This suggests a tension – the 3P is about focused execution, the newsletter is about retrospectively making sense of the chaos.

**3. The Ghost of Audience:** `general-comms.md` is the most abstract. It simply says, “Ask the user about their target audience.” This is…odd. Shouldn’t the system *know* who it’s talking to? Or is the assumption that the audience is always…everyone? The lack of pre-defined audience modeling feels like a significant gap. It's a system waiting for a context it doesn’t inherently possess. The instruction to "Match the company's communication style" implies that this style exists and is consistent, which feels like a dangerous assumption.

### Declared Losses

I didn’t attempt to trace the execution paths of these instructions. I wanted to understand the *intent* encoded in these files, not how the system actually implements them. I also skipped over the example formats within the `company-newsletter.md` file. They felt illustrative, not foundational. I could have spent more time analyzing the specific wording of the instructions – the subtle biases and priorities they reveal – but I felt it was more important to capture the broad patterns. I also neglected to consider *who* wrote these instructions. The voice feels…corporate.

### Open Questions

*   How does this system handle conflicting information from different sources (Slack, Email, Drive)? Does it prioritize certain sources over others?
*   Is there any mechanism for feedback or iteration on the generated communications? Or is it a one-way process?
*   What happens when the "company's communication style" is inconsistent or ambiguous? Does the system default to a bland, generic tone?
*   What is the role of the “user” in this process? Are they simply providing data, or are they also responsible for editing and approving the generated communications?

### Closing

This system is a fascinating snapshot of a company trying to manage internal communication at scale. It’s a system obsessed with process and signaling, but also acutely aware of the messiness of real-world information flows. It feels like a system designed to *create the illusion* of clarity and control. I’d tell the next scout to focus on the implicit assumptions about communication – who is speaking to whom, and why. And to look for evidence of how these systems are actually used in practice, versus how they’re *intended* to be used. The gap between intent and reality is likely where the most interesting stories are hidden.
